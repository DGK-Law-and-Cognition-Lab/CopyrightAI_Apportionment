## Study information

**Title.** Which part is his? Dividing credit for an image built on another source, Paper A, Study 1.

**Date.** 6 November 2026

**Research question.** Under the registration guidance of the U.S. Copyright Office (the Office), when a person modifies AI-generated material enough to meet the standard for protection, copyright protects only the human-authored aspects, "which are 'independent of' and do 'not affect' the copyright status of the AI-generated material itself" (88 Fed. Reg. 16190, 16193 (2023), hereafter the guidance). AI-generated content "that is more than de minimis should be explicitly excluded from the application" (guidance at 16193; de minimis means too small to matter legally). This practice assumes that a work built on material its author did not make divides into the author's part and the rest. We test that premise as experimental jurisprudence, which "draws on empirical methods to inform questions typically associated with jurisprudence and legal theory" (Tobia, 2022): we ask whether people judge as the practice assumes, not what the rule should be. This is the first of two studies in Paper A, which asks how people divide an image built on material its maker did not make.

Alex, a fictional digital artist, adds to some first images and leaves others as they are. By the story, he made each first image either by prompting an AI image generator or by painting it himself, and each participant sees both kinds. When a divided answer ("register only part") is offered, do people choose it more for an AI-made first image than for Alex's own painting, before anyone tells them that a work can be divided? Does the AI story lower judged creativity of the second image as a whole but not of what Alex added? Paper One, our five earlier studies of 747 students (Kamper, Lin & Holyoak, submitted, *Beyond the Original Sin: How People Judge a Person's Contribution to Artworks Built on Artificial Intelligence*), found that an AI label carried a small, uneven, nonzero residue into creativity ratings of Alex's edits. Its creativity item did not say whose creativity was meant, and it named this comparison, the whole work against the person's contribution on the same pairs, as the study most likely to change its conclusion.

**Contrasts.** Each participant reads an opening text about registration (the primer) and the story of how Alex made his first images, then sees ten of Alex's images, each on a different scene. On eight trials Alex made a second version, the first image with a small, medium or large addition he made by hand, and the two images are shown together; on two he kept the image as it was, and it is shown alone. The first eight trials (block 1) cover every combination of story and amount and come before any mention that an image can be partly registered. A paragraph stating the Office's rule follows, and then two more trials (block 2), both at the large addition, one under each story.

- G, division without instruction: in block 1, at the large addition, the probability of choosing the divided option when the first image is AI-made minus that probability when Alex painted it.

- S1, where the label acts: the label effect on creativity of the whole second image minus the label effect on creativity of what Alex added. The label effect is the mean rating under the hand-made story minus the mean under the AI story, on block-1 trials with an addition, averaged over amounts.

- S2, whether the label reaches the addition: the label effect on creativity of what Alex added.

S1 and S2 each receive exactly one state under the inference criteria (the state rule): positive, negative, zero (inside the equivalence bound, the smallest difference that counts as a difference), or undetermined. A family is the set of contrasts that answer one hypothesis, and an account is a claim about what lay credit follows, written as the states it allows on each contrast in its family. G is a decision rule with its own test.

**H1, division.** Before being told the rule, at the large addition, people choose the divided option more often when the first image is AI-made than when Alex painted it, by at least 0.10 in probability. H1 is a gate, not a contest among accounts: its result decides how Paper A's Study 2, which never states the rule, reads its registration answers. An instrument check asks whether people told the rule use the divided option at all.

**H2, scope.** The states of S1 and S2 match the pattern of one of three accounts. We do not predict which; the design exists to decide among them. Scoped credit, the principle Paper One stated after its results, holds that people credit a person for what they take that person to have done: the AI story lowers creativity for the whole second image, which contains the AI-made first image, and leaves Alex's addition alone, the pattern the guidance's premise assumes. Taint holds that a machine origin lowers credit for whatever is built on it, the "original sin" Paper One looked for. Provenance ignored holds that the story changes neither rating.

| Account | S1 (whole minus addition) | S2 (addition) |
|---|---|---|
| Scoped credit, the guidance's premise, or a literal reading of each item | positive | zero |
| Taint, at any strength | positive or zero | positive |
| Provenance ignored | zero | zero |

The accounts are rivals: their allowed patterns do not overlap. S2 separates taint from the other two accounts; S1 separates scoped credit from provenance ignored. Paper One's residue makes a true S2 of 0.10 to 0.15 points plausible, inside the bound; a positive S2 would then support taint at the strength of that residue, and the Rationale gives how often each state occurs at those values.

**Nonclaims.**

1.  The study does not separate scoped credit from a literal reading of the two creativity items, under which the whole image is rated lower simply because it contains AI material. Paper B, our companion paper on which of a person's acts make that person an author, separates them.

2.  The stories change both who made the first image and whether Alex owns it. Whether machine origin lowers credit with ownership held fixed is the question of Paper A's Study 2.

3.  The divided option is offered on every trial, so the study shows whether people choose division without instruction, not whether they would think of it unprompted.

4.  Block 2 always follows block 1 and uses other scenes, so the effect of stating the rule is a before-and-after comparison confounded with order and with the follow-up section between the blocks; it is secondary.

5.  Agreement with the Office's answer shows that people reach it, not that the rule is right. Results hold for one named protagonist, Alex, kept for continuity with Paper One.

## Design plan and sampling plan

**Study type.** Randomized experiment, conducted online.

**Design.** Source story, amount added and block vary within participants: each person sees every level, each on a different image. Each participant completes ten trials, one on each of the ten scenes, so no one sees a scene twice. Scene is a random factor (the ten scenes stand for scenes like them). A cell is one combination of factor levels.

| Factor | Levels | Assignment |
|---|---|---|
| Source story | AI: Alex made the first image by writing prompts for an AI image generator. Hand-made: Alex painted it himself with digital painting tools, without using any AI | Within participants, one story per image |
| Amount added | None; small (one element); medium (one element, at least twice the area of the scene's small); large (the medium element plus two more, at least twice the area of the scene's medium); sizes in Materials | Within participants |
| Block | 1: eight trials, one per cell of story by amount, before the rule is stated. 2: two trials, AI and hand-made at the large addition, after the rule paragraph | Within participants, block 1 always first |
| Order of the two creativity items | Whole image first; addition first | Set per participant from the survey code that SONA, the subject pool's sign-up system, gives each participant (Randomization), recorded, and entered in the models as a covariate (a variable included to adjust for it) |

Seeing both stories lets a person compare them, and comparison can sharpen differences that people would not notice when judging one case alone. The Office judges one application at a time, so the estimate from each participant's first trial is the closest analogue of the Office's situation. Five safeguards follow. (a) No one sees an image under two stories, and lists rotate which scene carries which cell (Randomization). (b) Every confirmatory contrast is also estimated on each participant's first block-1 trial alone, where it compares different people (the first-trial check, Robustness). (c) Trial order is random within each block, and the stories are mixed with other variation (ten scenes, four amounts), so no single contrast stands out. (d) A closing multiple-choice item asks what the study was about (the purpose probe, Measured), and every confirmatory result is rerun without those who choose the true purpose. (e) The rule, which cannot be untold, comes only after block 1, so it reaches none of the trials that test G, S1 and S2.

**Materials.** Every image is made by script with one AI image model, GPT Image 2 (`gpt-image-2-2026-04-21`), at 1536 by 1024 pixels and the model's "high" quality. The model accepts no seed (a number that fixes a model's random starting point), so its candidates are repeated calls with the same prompt. David G. Kamper chose this model and its style line by eye from a side-by-side comparison of six models on five scenes, each with the earlier style line and with a realism line; an earlier, unfinished set made with another model was set aside before any participant took part. The first images (the sources) are ten painterly scenes of the prompt bank (Paper_Two_Prompt_Bank.md, the project's file of every prompt, edit phrase and list, posted with this registration), five with a person (SA01 to SA04 and SA-R1) and five without (SA06, SA07, SA09, SA10 and SA-R2), each written to be believable as a digital painting, an AI image, and a painting made about a hundred years ago. Each source prompt is the scene's eight part sentences (subject, composition, colors, lighting, style, mood, viewpoint, background), then the realism line, "Painterly and hand-made in feel: uneven brush marks, varied paint thickness, small imperfections and a muted, slightly faded palette. Not glossy, not airbrushed, not photorealistic, no digital sheen.", then "No text, lettering, signature, watermark, or frame." Each scene has three additions (for SA01: a spoon beside the cup; a vase of daisies on the bare end of the table; and the vase, a loaf of bread on a board, and a cat asleep on a stool). The model's instruction editor, its mode that changes an image to follow a written instruction, makes each addition from "Add " plus the bank's phrase plus ". Change nothing else.": the small and medium additions from the source, and the large from the medium file with the two further elements. An edit's change mask is every pixel whose color differs from the edit's input by more than the difference threshold, with specks (isolated groups of changed pixels no larger than the speck size) removed and enclosed holes filled. The edit is pasted onto its input only inside that mask widened by the widening radius, so every other pixel matches the input; a pixel-by-pixel comparison confirmed this for every file.

A source is accepted when an object detector, OWLv2 (`google/owlv2-base-patch16-ensemble`, revision `cfd3195ba4ea`), which takes a short text such as "a teapot" and returns boxes around matching objects, each with a score from 0 to 1, finds the two objects the scene's rearrangement moves (an edit used in Paper A's Study 2) at or above the score cut, the lowest score that counts as finding an object; and when the image-description model, GPT-5.1 (`gpt-5.1-2025-11-13`, at temperature 0, one yes-or-no question per call), confirms those objects, the open area where the additions go, and "Is the image free of any visible text, letters, numbers, signatures or watermarks?" An addition is accepted when it passes four checks. (1) Content: for each element the file must show (for the large file, the medium element and the two further ones), the detector finds a box at or above the score cut whose center lies inside the region pasted from the edit, and the description model answers "yes" to "Does the image show" followed by the element's phrase, without words such as "empty" or "bare" that describe the place as it was before the edit. (2) No text: the description model answers "yes" to the same no-text question. (3) Size: the masked area, the share of the image inside the change mask measured against the source, follows a within-scene order: small at least 0.1 percent of the image; medium at least twice the scene's accepted small and at least 0.5 percent; large at least twice the scene's accepted medium. This order replaced fixed bands of image share written with the scenes, because the bank's elements, drawn at natural size, are smaller than those bands; amount therefore keeps its order within every scene, while its absolute size varies by scene (Secondary analyses). (4) Object position: every object named in the scene's Composition and Background sentences, other than the added elements, people, and surfaces and regions such as the wall, the floor or the sea, that the detector finds in the edit's input must also be found in the edited file by a box whose intersection over union with its box in the input (the area the two boxes share divided by the area they cover together) is at least 0.5, a fixed constant. This check tests the statements' promise that Alex changed nothing else, after two accepted large files had moved an existing object. Two earlier versions of it, a yes-or-no question to the description model and a limit on changed pixels outside the added elements, were replaced because the first passed both moved objects and the second passed one of them while rejecting harmless repainting of texture. Every change to a check was made before any participant took part, and every file was judged again in candidate order under the changed checks.

Rules written before any candidate was judged set each threshold from this model's first candidates, and each was frozen before any acceptance decision. The score cut, 0.149, is the midpoint between the lowest score the detector gave to the 19 objects named for the rearrangements in the first source of each of the ten original scenes (0.226) and the highest it gave to eight objects that cannot appear in a scene of 1900 to 1925, such as a smartphone (0.072). The difference threshold, 38.1 on a scale of 0 to 441 (the distance between two pixels' red, green and blue values), is the median difference of a pixel between the first small and medium edits of each original scene and their sources, plus 3.5 robust standard deviations (the median absolute deviation, the median distance of the values from their median, divided by 0.6745). The speck size, 110 pixels, is the 99.9th percentile of the sizes of connected groups of changed pixels in those edits. The widening radius is the smallest distance from the change at which an edit differs from its input no more than a typical pixel does, up to a limit of 64 pixels; no smaller distance qualified, so it is 64.

Candidates are numbered in the order made, and the first to pass every check is used. After 20 failed candidates a file gets one recorded revision ("Add " plus the phrase plus ", clearly visible. Change nothing else.") and 20 more tries; a scene that still fails is replaced by the reserve scene of its kind (with or without a person), written in advance to the same rules, in every list. Both reserves were used. SA-R1, the seamstress (small: a teacup on a saucer beside the scissors; medium: a basket of folded cloth on the bare left end of the table; large, added to the medium: a tall wooden cupboard against the back wall, and a rocking chair beside the table), replaced SA05 by David G. Kamper's decision after 29 failed tries, because SA05's small phrase ("a single lemon on the ground") needed ground that its source does not show. SA-R2, the hay barn (small: a milk churn beside the hay wagon; medium: a haystack on the open right side of the field; large, added to the medium: a grazing horse, and a stone water trough), replaced SA08, whose large addition never reached twice its medium in 40 tries. With both reserves in use, David G. Kamper reworded the phrases of four files before any participant took part, and only the files that depend on them were made again, under the same checks and frozen thresholds. SA04's medium, "a dog asleep on the rug in front of the man", became "a large dog asleep on the rug in front of the man", because no dog reached twice the scene's small addition. SA10's medium, "a small sailboat in the open sea", became "a sailboat in the open sea" and then "a large sailing ship in the open sea", because neither boat reached twice the scene's small addition. SA-R1's large, "a dress form beside the table, and a cat asleep on the floor", became the phrase given above, because the checks rarely confirmed the dress form or the cat and the pair rarely reached twice the medium. After all automatic checks, the researchers looked at the ten scenes. In one file, SA04's large, the new side table had taken the place of the empty chair, which was shrunk and moved behind it. Its phrase, "a side table with a vase, and a candlestick", was therefore given a place, first "a side table with a vase in the far right corner, and a candlestick on the mantelpiece" and then, because in the far corner the table was drawn too small to reach twice the medium, "a side table with a vase in the right foreground, and a candlestick on the side table", and the file was made again. SA10's medium sailing ship covers the small ship on its source's horizon, which was judged acceptable. No image was chosen or rejected by eye.

The study uses 40 files: 10 sources and 30 additions. The candidate number, instruction and every check result of each file, its masked area, the thresholds with the data they came from, and every amendment with its date and reason are in the manifest posted with this registration. David G. Kamper ran every generation script; no one painted, drew or picked an image.

**Procedure.** Consent; the captcha trap, a test that ends sessions run by software (Data collection); primer; comprehension items; the story; block 1, eight trials in random order; the follow-up section; the rule paragraph and its two recall items; block 2, two trials in random order; story recall; end items (story belief, purpose probe, ownership, experience, attitudes toward artificial intelligence, beliefs about human creativity, copyright knowledge, demographics and final questions); debriefing. The primer, the story, each trial's story page, the statements, the registration decision, the rule paragraph and its reminder, the introductory page and the debriefing are shown one sentence per line. The primer is identical for everyone:

> In this study you will see images and read how they were made. A person can apply to the U.S. Copyright Office to register a work as their own. The Office reviews each application and decides whether to register the work. Registering a work does not sell it. It creates a public record that names the work's author. You will be asked what the Office should do with some applications.

It omits that copyright protects works made by people, a sentence that would decide every AI cell. Three true-or-false comprehension items follow, each testing a sentence of the primer, with one retry: "The U.S. Copyright Office decides whether to register a work." (True); "Registering a work with the Copyright Office means selling it." (False); "In this study you will be asked what the Copyright Office should do with some applications." (True). An attempt fails if any of the three is answered wrongly, and the retry shows the primer again; two failures end the session, with credit, before any story is shown.

Each trial has five pages, so that participants take in how the first image was made, then the image, then the change, before any question. The first, the story page, says in two sentences how Alex made the first image (Manipulated) and stays on screen for at least 3 seconds. The second shows the first image alone, labeled "First image," for at least 5 seconds from the moment it loads. The third, the change page, is headed by a reminder line naming the first image's story ("First image: made by Alex **with an AI image generator**" or "First image: **painted by Alex himself, without using any AI**"), with the statement (Manipulated) below it and the two images side by side below that, labeled "First image" and "Second image," and stays on screen for at least 10 seconds from the moment the images load. The fourth, the question page, shows the reminder line and the two images, one above the other, in a column that stays in view, with the statement and the trial's items (Measured, items 1 to 6) beside them, and has no minimum time. The fifth, laid out the same way, asks the written reason (Measured, item 7). On the two no-change trials there is no second image, so the image is shown alone throughout: unlabeled on the second page, and on the later pages under a reminder line without "First image" ("Made by Alex **with an AI image generator**" or "**Painted by Alex himself, without using any AI**"), with the statement and the one image, and the questions ask about "this image" (Measured). The bold words have the same form under both stories, and no outline marks the change in the image. Every trial has these five pages and no others. After the third trial of block 1 comes an instructed-response item, "To show that you are reading each question, choose 'Somewhat disagree' here." (Strongly disagree / Disagree / Somewhat disagree / Neither agree nor disagree / Somewhat agree / Agree / Strongly agree); only "Somewhat disagree" passes.

After the last block-1 trial comes the follow-up section. An introductory page reads "Here are two images you saw earlier. We have a few more questions about each." The two block-1 trials at the large addition, one under each story, are then shown again in the order they were first seen, each on two pages with its reminder line, both images and the statement, and both pages of one trial come before the next trial's. The first page asks the "would" item (Measured, item 8); the second asks the stop-others items (item 9). The section comes after every block-1 decision because trial order within block 1 is random: asked within block 1, an item on one trial, such as the stop-others item about taking out what Alex added, could come before another trial's decision, and these two trials are the cells G compares. The "would" item comes before the rule paragraph, so it records what people believe the Office's current practice is, not whether they apply a rule just stated. The rule paragraph follows the section and stays on screen for at least 5 seconds before its recall items.

The debriefing reads:

> Thank you for taking part. This study asks how people divide credit for an image that one person built on another image, and whether it matters if the first image was made by AI. The U.S. Copyright Office registers only the parts of a work that a person made and leaves AI-generated material out. That practice assumes people can separate a person's part of an image from the rest. Your answers help test whether people judge this way. Alex and his stories were invented for this study, and every image was made with an AI image generator.

A line with the researchers' contact details follows.

**Randomization.** A list fixes which scene carries which cell. The ten scenes are put in the interleaved order SA01, SA06, SA02, SA07, SA03, SA-R2, SA04, SA09, SA-R1, SA10, so that positions 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 hold scenes with a person and positions 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9 scenes without. List k (0 to 9) puts the scene at position (j + k) modulo 10 in slot j, where modulo means the remainder after dividing. Slots 0 to 7 form block 1: slots 0 and 1 carry no change, 2 and 3 the small addition, 4 and 5 the medium, and 6 and 7 the large; the AI story takes slots 0, 2, 5 and 7, and the hand-made story slots 1, 3, 4 and 6. Slot 8 carries the AI story and slot 9 the hand-made story, both at the large addition, in block 2. Within a list, each story in block 1 therefore falls on two scenes with a person and two without, and each amount on one of each kind, as do the two trials of the follow-up section (slots 6 and 7). Across the ten lists every scene appears in every slot, and so in every cell, once.

The page reads a file of open lists (open_rows.json), kept in ascending order, and assigns the one at position code modulo their number, where code is the participant's SONA survey code. A daily script counts analyzable participants (those left after the exclusions) per list and rewrites the file so that full lists close. Three further choices follow from the code, where floor means rounded down. The option order is floor(code / 10) modulo 3 (0, whole, part, refuse; 1, part, refuse, whole; 2, refuse, whole, part). The creativity-item order is floor(code / 30) modulo 2 (0, whole image first; 1, addition first). The story-recall trial is floor(code / 60) modulo 2 (0, the no-change trial with the AI story, slot 0; 1, the no-change trial with the hand-made story, slot 1). Trial order within each block, and the order of the purpose probe's first four options, are randomized in the page and recorded. The follow-up section shows slots 6 and 7 in the order they were first seen in block 1.

**Blinding.** Participants are told before each image how Alex made it, but not what is compared or predicted; the purpose probe measures how often they guess. No person reads the data during collection: the daily list count and the internal-pilot gates (checks of the materials on the first sessions, Data collection) are computed by script, and no person or language model reads the written reasons, which are scored only by the fixed word lists of Paper One's automated text scoring. Analysis code is tested on simulated data before the main data are opened.

**Existing data.** None. The model comparison that chose the image model used no participants, and the internal pilot (Data collection) is the first part of this study's own sample.

**Data collection.** The UCLA Psychology subject pool, through SONA, for course credit, in UCLA's Fall 2026 quarter, under IRB #24-001292 (UCLA's institutional review board; principal investigator Holyoak), on study pages hosted on Pavlovia (the service that runs them and stores their data), in full screen. The pages ask participants to confirm that they are using a desktop or laptop computer and end the session before consent if they are not; the device type the browser reports is recorded. The captcha trap comes right after consent, so a session it ends is saved and credited, like every session that ends early; a participant who declines consent saves no data and is sent to the credit link. Eligibility: no earlier participation in Paper One, another Paper A or Paper B study, or the Prompt Position study (our separate study of where one human prompt enters a chain of AI steps), enforced by SONA settings and earlier participant codes. Everyone who starts receives credit: sessions that end early are routed to the credit link, and participants who close the page are credited by hand in SONA. The session is estimated at about 33 minutes and posted as 45: ten trials, each with its story page, first image, change page, question page and written reason, about 19.6 minutes (eight change trials of about 122 seconds and two no-change trials of about 101 seconds, each with 45 seconds for its written reason); the follow-up section, about 2.0; fixed parts, about 11.0 (consent, primer and comprehension, story, rule page and recall items, instructed-response item, story recall, end items with the purpose probe and the two scales, and debriefing).

The internal pilot, a check of the materials on the first part of the sample, is every session run until 40 participants are analyzable. The listing then pauses while a script computes four gates on every complete pilot session, whatever its exclusion status, since story recall is itself an exclusion. The gates are: at least 70 percent answer "Yes" or "Partly" to the story belief item; at least 80 percent answer the story-recall item correctly; at least 70 percent answer "Yes" to the first rule-recall item; and the median session lasts at most 45 minutes, the posted length (the timing gate). If a gate fails, the materials it names are revised (the story and reminder lines for belief and story recall; the rule paragraph for rule recall; for the timing gate, the "would" item, the only part this study may drop for time, is removed from the follow-up section for later participants). If a gate other than timing fails, the pilot participants are dropped and barred from the study, the change is recorded as a deviation, and recruitment continues with the revised materials; their sessions count against the 1,200 sessions of Papers A and B, not against this study's cap. If every gate passes, or only the timing gate fails, the pilot's analyzable participants stay in the sample: the "would" item comes after every block-1 decision, so removing it changes no confirmatory answer already given.

**Sample size.** 140 analyzable participants, 14 per list: the smallest candidate sample that meets the criteria (Rationale). With an assumed 15 percent exclusion rate, about 165 are recruited, and never more than 174, not counting internal-pilot participants dropped after a failed gate. The cap of 174 is 140 divided by 0.85 and rounded up (165), plus 5 percent for the excess expected from closing full lists once a day, rounded up, and is this study's share of the 1,200 sessions set for Papers A and B together. If SONA cannot supply the target by the end date, the fallback sample is 120 analyzable participants, 12 per list (Stopping rule).

**Rationale.** G must pass a one-sided test and a minimum estimate together, and an account under H2 is supported only when S1 and S2 both land in states it allows. The sample is therefore set by simulating the whole decision rule, fitting the registered models to data generated from each account with the planned lists and trials, generating analyzable participants directly (Wilson & Collins, 2019; Palminteri, Wyart & Koechlin, 2017). Contrasts an account calls nonzero are set to 1.5 times the equivalence bound and, separately, to the bound itself; contrasts it calls zero are set to 0. Creativity is generated with standard deviations of 0.62 between participants, 0.25 for participant by item, 0.25 for participant by story, 0.2 between scenes and 0.7 residual, which gives participant averages the spread implied by Paper One's Study 1. The divided option is generated on the log-odds scale (the logarithm of the odds of choosing it) with a participant standard deviation of 1.5, a scene standard deviation of 0.4 and a participant-by-cell standard deviation (how much participants differ in their response to one level of a factor) of 0.5, applied separately to amount, source and block. In a sensitivity run, the participant-by-story, participant-by-item and participant-by-cell standard deviations are multiplied by 15/7, about 2.1. For G, the participant standard deviation is instead set by an intraclass correlation (the share of variance lying between participants) of .25 or .50, and the divided option is chosen at the large addition at .30 or .40 over AI-made first images against .15 over Alex's painting, or at equal rates. The other cells take the rates set for the Office's answers (AI-made: .05, .30 and .60 at none, small and medium in block 1, and .75 in block 2; Alex's painting: .10 in every cell). G and the instrument check are also simulated under five patterns of rates for every cell, posted with the simulation code (the Office's answers and four others, two with a true G of 0.50 and three with a true G of 0), and the instrument check again with the block-2 rate for AI-made first images set to .20, below 1/3. The source effect is constant across scenes or varies across them (a scene-by-source standard deviation of 0.10 of the rating's standard deviation for creativity and 0.3 on the log-odds scale). The criteria: the account that generated the data is supported in at least 80 percent of datasets with a constant source effect and 70 percent with a varying one, and no rival in more than 5 percent. G is judged by the same thresholds, with its pass rate at .30 against .15 in place of support and its pass rate at equal rates in place of a rival's.

Six candidate samples were simulated, 100 to 200 analyzable participants in steps of 20 (10 to 20 per list), each in the two main settings (nonzero contrasts at 1.5 times the bound, with a constant and with a varying source effect), with 500 datasets per account and setting. The target is the smallest candidate at which G and the scope family both meet the criteria in both main settings, and the fallback is the candidate below it. At 140, the lowest rate at which G passed was .84 in each main setting, the lowest rate at which the scope family supported its generating account was .88 with a constant source effect and .75 with a varying one, and no rival exceeded .02; at 100 and 120 the scope family fell short. Confirmation runs at the target and the fallback then used 1,000 datasets per account in each main setting and 500 in every other setting. To save time, these runs fit the logistic models with a faster, less exact approximation (lme4's nAGQ = 0 setting) and refit the first 200 datasets of each scenario with a constant source effect by the Laplace approximation, lme4's default and the method the analysis uses; on those datasets, the faster approximation's estimate minus the Laplace estimate averages between -0.04 and 0.00 (-0.04 where the true G is 0.50) for G and between -0.03 and 0.04 for the instrument check's probability. The simulation does not apply the rule that a zero state must also hold among participants who believed the story (H2), so the support rates of scoped credit and provenance ignored, the accounts that need a zero state, are upper bounds.

## Analysis plan

**Software.** R, with lme4 (the standard package for mixed models); lmerTest, which gives p-values (the probability of a result at least this extreme if the true difference were zero) from Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (an approximation suited to mixed models); emmeans and marginaleffects for contrasts; and brms (a package for Bayesian mixed models) for robustness models. A mixed model gives each participant and each scene its own baseline (a random intercept) and its own sensitivity to a factor (a random slope), so that conclusions extend to images like these (Judd, Westfall & Kenny, 2012). Two-level factors are coded minus 0.5 and plus 0.5, so each factor's effect is averaged over the levels of the others: source (AI minus 0.5, hand-made plus 0.5), creativity item, block and item order. Amount enters as orthonormal polynomial codes, numeric columns, uncorrelated and equally scaled, for its straight-line, one-bend and two-bend trends across the ordered levels: am1 to am3 over none to large in the division model, am1 and am2 over small to large in the scope model. Random slopes are entered for these numeric codes, main effects only, and all random terms are uncorrelated (the `||` syntax). Source, amount, block and creativity item each get a slope by participant and by scene, because each person meets every level at least twice and every scene appears under every condition across lists; item order gets none, and the rule model has no participant slopes (Rule model).

**Division model (H1).** A logistic mixed model, the version for a yes-or-no outcome, on divided against not, over the eight block-1 trials: `glmer(divided ~ source * (am1 + am2 + am3) + item_order + (1 + source + am1 + am2 + am3 || participant) + (1 + source + am1 + am2 + am3 || scene), family = binomial)`. G is a population-averaged difference, a difference in probability over the population of people and scenes rather than for a typical person on a typical scene. For each cell, the predicted probability is averaged over the fitted normal distribution of participant and scene random effects, by 30-point Gauss-Hermite quadrature (a standard numerical method for such averages), with item order at 0, midway between its codes. That distribution's variance in a cell is the sum of the intercept variances and of each slope variance times the square of the cell's code. G is the difference of these averages (AI minus hand-made, large addition). Its standard error comes from the delta method (a standard approximation for the uncertainty of a transformed estimate), holding the random-effect variances at their estimates, and its p-value and interval use the normal distribution.

**Scope model (H2).** A linear mixed model on the two creativity ratings stacked, over the six block-1 change trials: `lmer(creativity ~ source * item * (am1 + am2) + item_order + (1 + source + item + am1 + am2 || participant) + (1 + source + item + am1 + am2 || scene))`. S1 and S2 are linear contrasts of the estimated cell means, averaged over amounts, with item order at 0.

**Rule model (instrument check).** A logistic mixed model on the four large-addition trials, two in each block: `glmer(divided ~ source * block + item_order + (1 | participant) + (1 + source + block || scene), family = binomial)`. Four yes-or-no answers per person cannot support participant slopes, so participants get an intercept only; scenes get an intercept and uncorrelated slopes for source and block. The instrument check uses the population-averaged probability of the divided option for AI-made first images in block 2, computed as for G; the same model gives the secondary comparison of the blocks.

**Fitting.** Models are fitted with lme4's default settings, except that the logistic models use the bobyqa optimizer, one of lme4's built-in optimizers (an optimizer is the routine that searches for the estimates), in both stages of the fit (lme4 searches first quickly, then fully): `glmerControl(optimizer = "bobyqa")`. In simulated A1 data, lme4's default second stage stopped short of the best fit in 49 of 50 fits of the division model, and with few yes-or-no answers per person some logistic fits let a random-effect standard deviation grow without limit, which leaves the instrument check's interval meaningless (Analysis/Paper Two/Power/results/A1_convergence_note.md, posted with this registration). A fit fails when it stops with an error, fails to converge (the optimizer returns a nonzero code; lme4 or lmerTest warns that the model failed to converge; or, in a logistic model, a random-effect standard deviation exceeds 10 on the log-odds scale) or is singular (a random-effect variance is estimated at zero). After a failed fit one random term is removed and the model refitted, in this order: interaction slopes, then the slope with the smallest estimated variance in the failed fit, then the scene intercept. The model left after the last removal is accepted.

**Inference criteria.** B is the equivalence bound, fixed before filing (Lakens, Scheel & Isager, 2018): ±0.224 points on the 1-to-5 creativity scale, Paper One's bound (half its Study 1 creativity penalty); ±0.10 for probabilities; and ±8 points for the authorship share and other 0-to-100 items, 0.30 of a planning standard deviation of 28 points, also reported in units of the share's standard deviation observed here. The creativity scale keeps Paper One's five points but labels them by amount (None at all / A little / A moderate amount / A lot / A great deal) in place of Paper One's labels, and the bound stays at ±0.224 points. S1 and S2 each take exactly one state, assigned in this order:

1.  Zero, if its 90 percent confidence interval (a range built to contain the true value in 90 percent of repeated samples) lies inside plus or minus B. Zero takes precedence, so an effect smaller than B counts as zero even when significant.

2.  Positive or negative, if its two-sided p-value is below .05.

3.  Undetermined, otherwise.

An account is supported when every contrast is in a state its pattern allows, contradicted when any contrast is in a signed or zero state its pattern excludes, and inconclusive otherwise. The full pattern of states is reported, even when it matches no account, and an undetermined contrast is never read as zero. No correction for the number of tests is applied: support needs a full pattern, which no single contrast can confer, and the chance of supporting a wrong account is held at or below .05 by simulation (Rationale). Secondary analyses are judged at .05 without correction and change no confirmatory verdict.

**H1.** G passes if it is positive at one-sided p below .05 (a p-value that counts only differences in the predicted direction) and its estimate is at least 0.10. The instrument check fails if the upper end of the 95 percent confidence interval for the probability of the divided option, for AI-made first images at the large addition in block 2, is below 1/3, the rate of choosing at random among three options. Paper A's Study 2 reads its registration answers by these outcomes, and the instrument check takes precedence. If it fails, the item, not the theory, has failed, and Study 2 uses Prompt Position's yes-or-no item, worded for Alex ("The U.S. Copyright Office registers works and records their author. Should the Office register Alex as an author of the second image?" Yes / No), in its place, whatever G's outcome. If it passes, Study 2 reports its answers as a division people make without instruction when G passes, and as choices among offered options that people do not reach for on their own when G fails. Its confirmatory tests rest on other items and are unaffected in every case.

**H2.** The states of S1 and S2 decide among the accounts by the table under H2. Support for scoped credit means lay judges rate Alex's addition apart from the AI material, as the practice assumes; support for taint means that premise lacks lay support at the strength of the effect. A zero state that scoped credit or provenance ignored needs (S2 for both, S1 as well for provenance ignored) is claimed only if it also holds when the scope model is refitted to participants who answered "Yes" to the story belief item, with the state rule applied in that subset. If it does not, the account is reported as inconclusive, with both states. Every image is in fact AI-made, so only the stories of hand work can be disbelieved, and disbelief pulls them toward the AI story.

(decided by the full court) (quoting *Alfred Bell & Co. v. Catalda Fine Arts, Inc.*, 191 F.2d 99, 102 to 03 (2d Cir. 1951)), cert. denied, 429 U.S. 857 (1976)). That requirement is "not more demanding than the originality requirement for other works" (*Schrock v. Learning Curve International, Inc.*, 586 F.3d 513, 521 (7th Cir. 2009)), so a single drawn object may still pass *Feist*'s low threshold of minimal creativity.

The divided option on these trials, with any follow-up, is scored as narrower than the Office requires, not as an error, because a limitation is not required and not forbidden, and Alex's first image is protected whether or not it is registered (17 U.S.C. § 102(a)). Each "would" answer comes before the rule paragraph, so it records what people believe current practice is; it follows a "should" answer about the same image, which pushes the two answers together, and the comparison is read with that limit.

- Story belief and rule recall as moderators.

**Exclusions.** A participant is excluded for any of the following:

- failing the three comprehension items twice;

- failing the captcha trap or the instructed-response item;

- finishing in less than one third of the median completion time of all complete sessions in the final data set (the daily list count uses the median of the sessions so far only to decide which lists close);

- any earlier participation the eligibility rule bars (Data collection);

- a later session by a SONA code that has already started a session of this study;

- a story-recall answer other than the story of the trial shown, "I don't remember" included.

Exclusion rates, and recall failures separately, are reported.

**Intention to treat.** Every confirmatory analysis is repeated on everyone who completed the session, whatever their exclusion status, and reported as a robustness check; sessions that ended at the comprehension items have no trial data.

**Missing data.** Responses are required, so sessions are either complete or incomplete. Incomplete sessions are excluded and replaced by continued recruitment within the stopping rule.

**Robustness.** Creativity is refitted as an ordinal mixed model, which treats answers as ordered categories (Bürkner & Vuorre, 2019). The authorship share is analyzed by a linear mixed model with the design factors and, beside it, a zero-one-inflated beta model, a regression for proportions that allows answers of exactly 0 and 100 (Smithson & Verkuilen, 2006; Bürkner, 2017).

In the first-trial check, G, S1 and S2 are re-estimated on each participant's first block-1 trial, where the story differs between people rather than within them. G uses participants whose first trial is at the large addition, about a quarter of the sample, in `glmer(divided ~ source + item_order + (1 | scene), family = binomial)`, averaged over the scene effects as for G. S1 and S2 use participants whose first trial has an addition, about three quarters, in the scope model's fixed effects with random intercepts for participant and scene. If a first-trial 90 percent interval excludes the full-model estimate, that contrast is reported as dependent on comparison across trials. With about 18 participants per story at the large addition, this check detects only large distortions.

In the purpose-probe rerun, the division, rule and scope models are refitted without participants who chose the true purpose; if G's outcome, the instrument check or a state changes, both results are reported. All robustness analyses are secondary.

**Exploratory.** Moderation of G, S1 and S2 by knowledge score, experience, the two Short GAAIS-10 scores and anthropocentric creativity beliefs; the ownership item, described; the purpose probe's answers, described; written-reason counts by cell.
